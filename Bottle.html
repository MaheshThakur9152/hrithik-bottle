A shape that holds still what won’t.

Water, wine, ink, medicine, milk. A narrow neck, a stable base and a seal have carried liquids for thousands of years. Pick a material and see what changes.

Four parts do all the work

Every bottle, from a perfume vial to a wine magnum, is the same idea at different sizes. The neck slows the pour, the shoulder guides it, and the base keeps it upright.

Finish
The rim and threads at the top. It decides which cap, cork or crown fits.
Neck
The narrow tube that controls how fast liquid leaves and how little air gets in.
Shoulder
The curve where the neck widens. It carries the weight and gives a smooth pour.
Body and base
The storage space and the foot. Many glass bottles have a dimple underneath that adds strength.

Five moments in bottle history

  • Before glassPeople carried liquids in gourds, animal skins and fired clay.
  • First century BCEGlassblowing appears in the Roman world, so hollow glass vessels can be made quickly and in volume.
  • 1903Michael Owens builds an automatic bottle-making machine, ending the era of every bottle being blown by hand.
  • 1960s–70sEngineers at DuPont develop PET plastic bottles for fizzy drinks; a key patent followed in 1973.
  • TodayBillions are produced each year, and the question has shifted from how to make them to how many times to use each one.

The best bottle is the one you refill

Refill

A sturdy steel or glass bottle used for years replaces hundreds of single-use ones.

Recycle

Glass and aluminium can be melted and remade many times. Rinse them and keep caps with the right stream for your local scheme.

Return

Deposit schemes bring bottles back to be washed and filled again. Check whether your area has one.
